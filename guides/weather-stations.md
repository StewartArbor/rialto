<p class="eyebrow">ENVIRONMENT</p>
<h1 class="page-title">A weather station begins with where you put the sensor</h1>

<p>A station may advertise ten sensors, but a badly placed thermometer still measures a sun-heated wall rather than the air. The useful chain is sensor → placement → logging interval → interpretation.</p>

<h2>Temperature and humidity</h2>
<p>Outdoor sensors need protection from direct solar radiation and strong thermal influence from walls and roofs. Indoors, a reading beside a radiator, window, humidifier, or kitchen is a reading of that micro-location.</p>

<h2>Pressure</h2>
<p>A barometer is useful for trends. For comparison with official observations, check whether the display reports station pressure or a sea-level-adjusted value.</p>

<h2>Rain and wind</h2>
<p>These measurements are particularly vulnerable to siting. A rain gauge beneath a tree and an anemometer between buildings can produce exquisitely precise numbers about the wrong environment.</p>
